Module 01

Linear Power Supply

A linear power supply converts AC mains voltage to a stable regulated DC output using a transformer, rectifier, filter, and voltage regulator — with low noise but moderate efficiency.

Block Diagram — Linear Power Supply

Stage-by-Stage Breakdown

🔌

① Transformer

Steps down 230V AC to a safer low voltage (e.g., 12V AC). The turns ratio N₁/N₂ = V₁/V₂ determines the output voltage. Core: laminated iron or ferrite.

⚡

② Rectifier

Converts AC to pulsating DC. A bridge rectifier (4 diodes) gives full-wave rectification — both half-cycles are utilized, giving Vdc = 0.636 × Vm.

〜

③ Filter

Electrolytic capacitors smooth the pulsating DC. Larger capacitance → lower ripple voltage. Ripple factor r = 1/(4√3 · f · C · RL).

🔋

④ Regulator

IC regulators (78xx series for fixed, LM317 for variable) maintain constant output despite load/line variations. Uses a series-pass transistor in linear mode.

Signal Stages — Live
━ AC Input ━ After Rectifier ━ After Filter ━ Regulated DC
Vripple = IL / (2 × f × C)    [Full-Wave]
ηlinear = Vout / Vin × 100%
⚠️
Efficiency Limitation Linear supplies dissipate the voltage difference (Vin − Vout) as heat. For a 12V input regulated to 5V, about 58% of power is wasted as heat — hence the need for heatsinks.

Module 02

Switch Mode Power Supply (SMPS)

SMPS achieves high efficiency (80–95%) by switching the input voltage at high frequency (20 kHz–2 MHz) instead of dissipating excess as heat — enabling compact, lightweight designs for modern electronics.

Block Diagram — SMPS (Flyback Topology)

How PWM Controls Output Voltage

The PWM controller varies the duty cycle D of the switching transistor. Higher D → more energy transferred → higher output voltage. The feedback loop (via optocoupler for isolation) adjusts D to maintain regulation.

Vout = D × Vin    [Buck Converter]
D = ton / T    (Duty Cycle, 0 to 1)
Efficiency η = Pout / Pin ≈ 80–95%
ℹ️
Why High Frequency? Operating at 100 kHz–1 MHz allows transformers and inductors to be 1000× smaller than 50 Hz equivalents (core size ∝ 1/f), dramatically reducing weight and size.

Common SMPS Topologies

Step-Down

Buck Converter

Vout < Vin. Used in CPUs, battery chargers. Series inductor stores energy; freewheeling diode allows current flow during off-time.

Step-Up

Boost Converter

Vout > Vin. Used in LED drivers, PFC circuits. Inductor pumps energy into output capacitor during off-time.

Isolated

Flyback Converter

Provides galvanic isolation via HF transformer. Multiple output voltages possible. Used in phone chargers, ATX power supplies.

Module 03

Uninterruptible Power Supply (UPS)

A UPS provides emergency power when the main supply fails, ensuring continuity for critical loads such as servers, medical equipment, and industrial control systems.

Block Diagram — Online Double-Conversion UPS

Type 1

Offline / Standby UPS

Normally passes mains directly to load. Switches to battery inverter in 4–10ms on failure. Low cost, low efficiency impact. Used for PCs, home appliances.

Switchover time: 4–10 ms

Type 2

Line-Interactive UPS

AVR (Auto Voltage Regulator) taps transformer to handle voltage sags/surges without switching to battery. Switchover: 2–4ms. Popular for network equipment.

Switchover time: 2–4 ms

Type 3

Online Double-Conversion

Load always powered from inverter. AC→DC→AC. Zero transfer time. Highest protection. Used for data centers, medical equipment, industrial control.

Switchover time: 0 ms

Module 04

Electrical Hazards & Precautions

Understanding electric hazards is fundamental to safe practice. Human body resistance, current path, and exposure duration determine the severity of injury.

Effect of Current on Human Body (IEC 60479)

⚡
Ohm's Law Reminder — It's the Current, Not Voltage, That Kills Skin resistance (dry): 100kΩ–1MΩ. Wet skin: as low as 1kΩ. At 230V with wet hands: I = 230/1000 = 230 mA — well into the fatal range. Always treat wet conditions with maximum caution.

Types of Electrical Hazards

⚡

Electric Shock

Current passing through the body. Effects depend on magnitude, path (hand-to-hand most dangerous — passes through heart), duration, and frequency.

🔥

Electrical Burns

Arc flash temperatures can reach 19,000°C. Entry/exit wounds from high-current shock. Internal burns along current path are often more severe than surface burns.

💥

Arc Flash / Blast

Ionized plasma from short-circuit arcs radiates intense UV, IR, and pressure waves. Can cause blindness, hearing damage, and severe burns up to several meters.

🔥

Electrical Fire

Overloaded cables, poor connections, and arcing generate heat. Wiring insulation degrades → short circuit → fire. Use fire-retardant cables and proper ratings.

💡

Electromagnetic Hazards

EMF from high-current conductors, RF interference in sensitive equipment. Shielding and grounding minimize exposure.

🛡️

Precautions & PPE

Insulated tools, rubber gloves (Class 00–4), arc-flash suits, safety goggles. Always follow LOTO (Lockout-Tagout) procedures before maintenance.

Module 05

Earthing (Grounding)

Earthing connects exposed conductive parts to the general mass of earth, ensuring fault currents flow safely to earth — protecting users, equipment, and triggering protective devices.

Pipe Earthing System

Types of Earthing Systems

Most Common

Pipe Earthing (IS 3043)

GI pipe (50mm dia, 2.5–3m long) buried vertically. Surrounds with charcoal and salt to reduce soil resistivity. Earth resistance ≤1Ω for large installations, ≤5Ω for domestic.

Rocky Soil

Plate Earthing

GI plate (600mm × 600mm × 6mm) or copper plate (600×600×3mm) buried at ≥3m depth. Used where pipe earthing is not feasible.

Large Networks

Strip / Rod Earthing

GI strip (25×1.6mm) or copper strip buried in trenches. Rod earthing uses driven rods in a ring pattern around substations.

Rearth = ρ / (2πL) × ln(4L/d)    [Dwight's Formula]
ρ = soil resistivity (Ω·m), L = electrode length, d = diameter

MCB — Internal Construction

⚙️
Two Trip Mechanisms in MCB Thermal (Bimetal): Bends on sustained overload (slow, inverse time). Magnetic (Solenoid): Instantaneous trip on short circuit current (typically >3×–10× In). MCBs are resettable — unlike fuses.

Derating Factors Cables in conduit, high ambient temp, or grouped with other cables must be derated. E.g., 3 cables in conduit at 40°C ambient: factor ≈ 0.75 × rated current.

Types of Wires & Cables

Domestic

PVC Insulated Wire

Polyvinyl chloride insulated copper conductors. Flexible, moisture-resistant. Used in house wiring, up to 1100V grade. IS 694 compliant.

Industrial

XLPE Armoured Cable

Cross-linked polyethylene insulation, steel wire armour (SWA). Excellent thermal resistance, used for underground and high-temp industrial installations (up to 90°C).

Outdoor

Overhead Conductor (ACSR)

Aluminium Conductor Steel Reinforced. Galvanized steel core for mechanical strength; aluminium strands for conductivity. Used in HT/LT overhead lines.

Fire-Safe

LSZH / FRLS Cable

Low Smoke Zero Halogen / Fire Retardant Low Smoke. Does not emit toxic gases when burning. Mandatory in tunnels, hospitals, public buildings.

Control

Multicore Flexible Cable

Multiple fine stranded conductors (Class 5/6 flexibility). Used for portable appliances, control panels. 300/500V to 450/750V grades.

Underground

Power Cable (11kV/33kV)

XLPE insulated, screened, SWA, PVC outer sheath. For underground HT distribution. Paper-insulated lead-sheathed (PILC) in older systems.

Billing Components

⚡

Energy Charge (kWh)

Variable component based on actual consumption. Slab-based tariff penalizes high consumers. Units = (Reading₂ − Reading₁).

📋

Fixed / Demand Charge

Flat monthly charge for maintaining the connection regardless of usage. Demand charge for industrial: based on peak kVA demand recorded by meter.

🏛️

Taxes & Surcharges

Electricity Duty (state tax), Fuel Adjustment Charge (FAC), Power Factor Penalty (PF < 0.9 attracts surcharge), TOD premium charges.
